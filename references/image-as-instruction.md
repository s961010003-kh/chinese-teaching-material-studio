# Image as instruction

## Core standard

A prominent image belongs in teaching material only when it changes what learners attend to, predict, compare, infer, remember, or produce. Beauty and topical relevance are secondary benefits, not the teaching function.

Plan each important image with four fields:

1. **Teaching function** — What cognitive move should the image enable: orient, notice, predict, compare, sequence, infer, feel a consequence, retrieve, or transfer?
2. **Reveal timing** — What is visible now, what is withheld, and when does the next clue appear?
3. **Student action** — What will learners point out, mark, order, say, write, debate, or revise while viewing it?
4. **Learning evidence** — What observable response will show that the image improved understanding?

If these fields cannot be completed concretely, make the image subordinate or omit it.

## Scene → action → framework → evidence

- **Scene:** make a setting, action, emotion, viewpoint, contrast, or consequence perceptible without embedding the answer as text.
- **Action:** require a small learner move before explanation: identify three clues, predict the unseen cause, arrange moments, choose a viewpoint, or name a change.
- **Framework:** convert perception into an editable and exact structure such as evidence–inference, before–after, symbol–meaning, claim–evidence–reasoning, timeline, or competing interpretations.
- **Evidence:** capture a response that can be checked: a cited detail, revised prediction, completed relation, spoken justification, or transfer to a new example.

Do not let the framework merely repeat the image. It should formalize, challenge, compare, or extend what the image helped learners notice.

## Useful visual teaching moves

- **Attention control:** composition, cropping, contrast, scale, or isolation directs learners toward the relevant clue before labels appear.
- **Productive suspense:** present an unresolved scene or partial consequence, ask for a prediction, then reveal the missing element on a later slide.
- **Emotional turn:** use a change of scene, distance, light, or consequence to make a textual turning point felt; follow it with evidence-based interpretation.
- **Controlled comparison:** keep viewpoint, scale, or layout stable while changing one meaningful variable.
- **Return and revision:** revisit an opening image after instruction and ask learners to state what they now see differently.
- **Retrieval cue:** reuse a restrained crop or visual motif only when it cues recall of a learned relationship; avoid repetition that is merely decorative.

## Design principles in service of learning

- Apply figure–ground, hierarchy, contrast, proximity, alignment, and signal-to-noise so the intended clue is found in the intended order.
- Use progressive disclosure when showing all information at once would remove prediction or discovery.
- Use picture superiority for concrete scenes, objects, actions, and consequences; use editable language for definitions, quotations, distinctions, and exact reasoning.
- Use isolation sparingly for one decisive word, image detail, or turning point.
- Build a strong opening question and a meaningful final return so the visual sequence has a memorable peak and closure.

## Storyboard table

Before generation, create a compact table with one row per important image:

| Scene or asset | Teaching function | Reveal timing | Student action | Learning evidence | Framework that follows |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example: person passes a flowering tree | Notice missed attention | Show before poem ending | Point to three clues of “missing” | Cites gaze, direction, and distance | Action–consequence mapping |

Generate distinct images only for distinct instructional moments. A single image may return as a retrieval cue or before/after comparison, but record why the repetition matters.

## Review gate

Before delivery, inspect the deck or handout and verify:

- every prominent image has a documented teaching function;
- learners act before the teacher supplies the intended interpretation when discovery is useful;
- the visual clue is legible from the expected viewing distance;
- image and editable framework do different but connected jobs;
- generated imagery contains no accidental text or misleading historical claim;
- visual drama does not overstate evidence or predetermine a legitimately open interpretation;
- the final task requires retrieval, justification, revision, or transfer rather than recognition alone.

When reporting the result, explain which images perform which teaching tasks and identify any imagery that is a teaching reconstruction rather than documentary evidence.
